The Hydrosphere

Look at Earth from space, directly above the center of the Pacific Ocean, and you would barely see any land at all. About seven-tenths of Earth’s surface is covered by water.

All the water on Earth, as one system

Most of the water is found in Earth’s oceans. Water is also found in streams, rivers, ponds, and lakes. Cracks and spaces in underground rocks and soil contain water, too. So does the air.

Most of Earth’s water is liquid. However, some is solid, frozen in ice caps and other glaciers. Some is an invisible gas, called water vapor, in the atmosphere. Altogether, all the water on Earth makes up a system called the hydrosphere.

hydrosphere n.
the Earth system that contains all of the water on Earth
Word part
The word part hydro- means “water.”
Words to know
A system consists of parts that interact. To interact means to have an effect on something else.

The Pacific Ocean

⅓

The Pacific covers approximately one-third of Earth’s surface. This is where Earth’s ocean is deepest.

The average depth is about 13,000 feet (4,000 meters). At its deepest point, in a far western area, the ocean floor is about 36,000 feet (11,000 meters) below the surface. That is almost seven miles! This section is called the Challenger Deep. Because it is so deep, it is mostly unexplored by scientists.

Seas, gulfs, and straits

Seas and gulfs

Specific regions of oceans. They are defined by land that borders or partially surrounds them.

Straits

Larger regions of ocean water are connected by narrow bodies of water between landmasses. These narrow waterways are called straits.

An example

The Gulf of Mexico, the Caribbean Sea, the Straits of Florida, and the Atlantic Ocean are all connected.

Why is the ocean salty?

Oceans, seas, and gulfs all contain salt water: water that contains dissolved salt. Most of the salt, the chemical sodium chloride, is the same as table salt! Some bodies of water are saltier than others.

1 · RocksThe salt comes from rocks on Earth’s land surfaces.
2 · RiversIt is picked up by streams and rivers as they flow toward the ocean.
3 · EvaporationAs water evaporates from the ocean into the air, the salt is left behind.
4 · TimeOver very long periods of time, this process has made the oceans salty.

Earth’s hydrosphere contains fresh water

Oceans are only one part of the hydrosphere. Water also occurs in streams, rivers, ponds, and lakes. These bodies of water lie on Earth’s land surfaces.

Groundwater

Water beneath Earth’s surface, in the spaces between rocky material.

Reservoirs

Places where water collects, in all its states. Earth’s main water reservoir is the ocean, which contains salt water. But most of Earth’s inland reservoirs contain fresh water.

Fresh water

Any water that has low amounts of salt in it. Rivers, streams, and wetlands all contain fresh water.

Not all lakes are fresh

Some groundwater and water in lakes are salt water instead of fresh water. The Great Salt Lake in Utah is one example. It formed when water carrying salts from rocks flowed into the lake. The lake has nowhere for the water to flow out. When water in the lake evaporates, salt is left behind. Over time, the salt accumulated, making the water very salty.

Part of the lake is a pinkish color. Its concentration of salt is much higher than that found in the world’s oceans.

Frozen fresh water

Some of the fresh water on Earth is liquid, but most fresh water is frozen in glaciers. The ice caps at the North and South Poles are two very large glaciers. Much of this water is permanently frozen. However, some polar ice thaws and refreezes with the seasons. When this freshwater polar ice melts, it can make the ocean water around it less salty. Because of recent warming of Earth’s climate, more polar ice melts each year than refreezes.

Where fresh meets salt

Fresh water from rivers flows into larger bodies of water. Rivers flow into lakes, oceans, gulfs, and seas. Where ocean water backs up into freshwater streams and rivers, the water becomes brackish. Brackish water has more salt than fresh water but not as much salt as ocean water.

Water has physical properties

Water can be found in nature as a solid, a liquid, or a gas. This is because water’s physical properties cause it to evaporate, condense, freeze, and melt within a relatively close range of temperatures.

212°F

Water boils at 212°F (100°C).

32°F

Water freezes at 32°F (0°C).

Evaporation

Water evaporates, or changes to a gas, very quickly at the boiling point. But it can evaporate at lower temperatures when heat energy is present. Water can even evaporate when it is frozen.

Condensation

Water changes from a gas (water vapor) back to a liquid. Water vapor condenses when it cools. This can also happen at different temperatures. Condensation on the outside of a glass of ice water happens when water vapor in the air contacts the cold glass.

Water vapor, water as a gas, is invisible. But it can be present together with solid and liquid water, as in a cold, snowy landscape with open water and mist.

Water in the atmosphere

Earth’s atmosphere contains water that enters the air as water vapor. As the water vapor rises into the air, it encounters cooler temperatures. This may cause the water vapor to condense into liquid water. Clouds are tightly packed droplets of liquid water. Eventually, these droplets fall back to Earth as precipitation. The form it takes usually depends on the temperature of the air near Earth’s surface.

Because wind pushes the clouds around, water is deposited in new places on Earth when it falls. The water that you see in a lake or river was once water vapor in the atmosphere. The water that falls to Earth continuously replenishes Earth’s ocean, lake, and groundwater reservoirs. And water evaporates from surface reservoirs to become water vapor again. The sun provides the heat energy that causes water from a lake’s surface to evaporate into the atmosphere.

The water cycle

The hydrosphere is constantly moving and changing. Evaporation, wind, and precipitation move water from one water reservoir to another. Gravity also moves water. Its pull causes water to flow downward. Currents in Earth’s oceans continuously move the water, too.

water cycle n.
the dynamic movement of water on, below, and above Earth’s surface

As water moves, the hydrosphere interacts with Earth’s land, air, and living things. In addition to changing position, water in the water cycle is constantly changing states.

Counting

I can count forward, count back, and skip count.

Learn

What is counting?

Counting means saying numbers in order. When you count things, touch each one and say one number. The last number you say tells how many.

Count forward

Numbers get bigger by one each time: 3, 4, 5, 6

Count backward

Numbers get smaller by one each time: 9, 8, 7, 6

Skip count

Skip counting means jumping over numbers.

  • By 2s: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10. These are all even numbers.
  • By 5s: 5, 10, 15, 20. The last digit is always 5 or 0.
  • By 10s: 10, 20, 30, 40. The last digit is always 0.

For the grown-up

What this lesson covers: counting forward and backward, one-to-one counting (each object gets exactly one number), the idea that the last number said tells how many, and skip counting by 2s, 5s and 10s.

  • Have your child touch objects as they count. Moving counted objects to a "done" pile helps.
  • If they lose their place, they can start again. Counting strategy matters more than speed.
  • Ask "What comes next?" during everyday moments: stairs, snacks, steps to the door.
  • Skip counting is early groundwork for multiplication, so use the 100 chart to spot the patterns.

Read, write, compare and order

I can read numbers, say which is bigger, and put numbers in order.

Learn

Three ways to show a number

Every number can be shown as a numeral (like 36), as a word (thirty-six), and with blocks (3 tens and 6 ones).

Comparing two numbers

Bigger numbers are greater than smaller ones. Smaller numbers are less than bigger ones. When they match, they are equal.

  • > means greater than: 8 > 5
  • < means less than: 5 < 8
  • = means equal to: 6 = 6

Think of a hungry alligator 🐊. Its mouth always opens toward the bigger number.

Tricks for bigger numbers

  • Look at the tens first. More tens means a bigger number: 52 > 47.
  • If the tens are the same, look at the ones: 63 > 61.
  • Ordering means putting numbers in a line from smallest to biggest, or biggest to smallest.

Tens and ones

I know what each digit in a number means.

Learn

Place value

Big numbers are built from tens and ones. A ten is a stick made of 10 little cubes. A one is a single cube.

Two digits, two jobs

In 47 the 4 is in the tens place. It means 4 tens, or 40. The 7 is in the ones place. It means 7 ones.

47 = 4 tens + 7 ones = 40 + 7

Make a trade

10 ones = 1 ten. When you have 10 ones, you trade them for a ten stick. That is why we never write more than 9 in the ones place.

Zero holds a place: in 50 there are 5 tens and 0 ones.

For the grown-up

What this lesson covers: the idea that a two-digit number is made of tens and ones, and that 10 ones can be traded for 1 ten. This is the single most important idea in Grade 1 number sense and it supports every later addition and subtraction strategy.

  • If you have them at home, use real objects: bundle 10 straws or pasta pieces with a rubber band to make a "ten".
  • Ask: "How many tens? How many ones? So what number is that?"
  • Watch for children who read 47 as "4 and 7". Guide them to say "4 tens and 7 ones, forty-seven".

Number tools

I can use ten-frames, number lines and counters.

Learn

Tools that help us see numbers

Mathematicians use tools to make numbers easier to see.

  • A ten-frame has 10 boxes in 2 rows of 5. Fill the boxes with counters. You can see the number without counting one by one, and you can see how many more make 10.
  • A number line shows numbers in order. Hop right to add. Hop left to take away.
  • Counters are small round chips. Use two colours to show two parts that make a number, like 6 red and 4 yellow make 10.

Numbers from 11 to 19 are 10 and some more. A full ten-frame plus 4 more counters is 14.

For the grown-up

What this lesson covers: using ten-frames, number lines and counters to represent numbers to 20, seeing number pairs that make 10, and understanding teen numbers as "10 and some more".

  • Number pairs that make 10 (1 and 9, 2 and 8, and so on) are the most useful facts to know by heart.
  • Use two colours of any small objects at home, such as coins, buttons or bricks, to explore all the ways to make 10.
  • Let your child point at the frame rather than counting one by one. Fast "seeing" of 5 and 10 is the goal.

Halves and quarters

I can share things into equal parts.

Learn

Equal parts

A fraction is a part of a whole. For parts to count, they must be equal. That means the same size. Fair sharing is equal sharing!

Halves

Cut a whole into 2 equal parts. Each part is one half. Two halves make one whole.

Half of 8 cookies is 4 cookies, because 4 + 4 = 8.

Quarters

Cut a whole into 4 equal parts. Each part is one quarter. Four quarters make one whole. Quarters are also called fourths.

A quarter of 8 cookies is 2 cookies, because 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 8.

For the grown-up

What this lesson covers: understanding that fractions need equal parts, naming halves and quarters of shapes, and finding halves and quarters of small sets of objects.

  • Fold paper, cut sandwiches and share snacks. Ask "Are the parts fair?"
  • Children often think any two pieces make halves. Show an unequal split and ask if it is fair.
  • Point out that two quarters together make one half. It is an early taste of equivalent fractions.
